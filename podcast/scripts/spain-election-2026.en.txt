Spain's November 29th election: the question isn't who wins, it's whether Vox joins the cabinet.
Here is the short answer. Pedro Sánchez's eight years in power will probably end on November 29th, and the People's Party leader, Alberto Núñez Feijóo, will probably be the next prime minister. But the People's Party cannot reach the 176 seats it needs on its own. It needs the far-right party Vox. So the real question is not who wins but how. Will Vox support a new government from outside, or will it sit in Spain's national cabinet for the first time? My view is that the second is slightly more likely.
First, why there is an early election at all. What cut this parliament short was housing. In September, the eviction of an 87-year-old by a real-estate company set off protests across Spain. On October 2nd, Sánchez's housing relief decrees were defeated in Congress, with the decisive votes coming from the Catalan party Junts. On October 5th, Sánchez called a snap election.
The campaign has not followed his script. On October 28th, a high-speed train crash near Urda, in the province of Toledo, killed 69 people. It was Spain's second fatal high-speed crash this year, and the transport minister, Óscar Puente, later resigned. Corruption cases are still unfolding, and on November 11th there was a general strike.
Now the polls. As the campaign opened, 40dB gave the People's Party 126 seats, the Socialists 118 and Vox 64. That puts the two parties of the right at 190 seats, 14 above a majority. Sigma Dos has them at 189 to 202, and GAD3 at 199. Compared with 2023, the People's Party is roughly flat, the Socialists fall from 121 seats to just over 100, and Vox roughly doubles, from 33 seats to the mid-60s. Nearly all the growth on the right belongs to Vox.
The one outlier is the state pollster, the CIS. It gives the Socialists 134 to 143 seats, and has the People's Party and Vox reaching at most 171, short of a majority. But the CIS is chaired by a former senior Socialist, and in 2023 it overestimated the left and underestimated Vox. I give it little weight, but not zero.
The Socialists have another problem: the left has split. Sumar was rebuilt as the Broad Front, led by Ada Colau, while Podemos is running on its own. Together they won 31 seats in 2023. This time the polls give them somewhere between 5 and 15. With small constituencies and proportional representation, a divided left wastes a lot of votes. That is the arithmetic behind the right's comfortable path to 176.
The coalition maths is simple. The mainstream polls put the People's Party's central estimate between 126 and 136 seats, far from 176, and Feijóo has ruled out relying on a Socialist abstention. So he almost certainly needs Vox. Feijóo says his goal is a single-party government with Vox's support in parliament, but that whether there is a coalition "does not depend on me". He has not ruled out giving Vox a key ministry such as the Interior, Foreign Affairs or Defence.
I think a coalition is more likely, for three reasons. First, the People's Party already governs with Vox in four regions. Second, if Vox really wins 60-plus seats, it is the only route to a majority, and the more seats it has, the less it will settle for voting from outside. Third, on the question of who voters prefer as prime minister, Abascal and Feijóo are now level, and joining the cabinet is Vox's chance to prove it can govern.
Here are my forecasts, as of November 13th.
One. Feijóo wins an investiture vote by the end of January: 80 percent. Within that, a People's Party and Vox coalition, 45 percent, and a People's Party minority government with Vox support from outside, 32 percent. Sánchez re-elected: 10 percent. Still deadlocked at the end of January: 10 percent. The signal to watch: if on election night Vox clears 65 seats and the People's Party stays below 130, a coalition becomes much more likely.
Two. The People's Party is the largest party: 88 percent. It wins between 120 and 134 seats: 55 percent. The People's Party and Vox together reach 176: 80 percent, and reach 190: 45 percent. Vox wins at least 60 seats: 65 percent. The Socialists win at least 100: 85 percent. Podemos wins one seat or none: 85 percent.
Three. Turnout above 2023's 70.4 percent: 65 percent. Turnout of 75 percent or more: 25 percent.
Four. A repeat election because nobody is elected within two months: 5 percent. If the Socialists lose power, Sánchez steps down as party leader by the end of June: 50 percent.
In one sentence: this election will probably end the Sánchez era, but it is more likely to be remembered as the moment Vox entered Spain's national government. The forecast I am most confident in is that, whoever governs, Vox will come close to doubling its seats. The one thing most likely to prove me wrong is that the CIS is right, and the private polls are systematically underestimating the Socialists.
Dates to watch: the Feijóo and Sánchez head-to-head debate on November 16th, the four-way debate on November 26th, election day on November 29th, the new parliament opening on December 23rd, and the investiture vote in January.
This is personal reasoning based on public reporting, and the probabilities are subjective. We'll come back on November 30th, and again in January, to check them.
