Axial Age Essays (II): Suffering, Samsara, and Calm by the Sea.
In the last essay I said that the Indians of the Axial Age went furthest of all, asking point-blank how the individual relates to the cosmos. I've always wondered what pushed them to think so deeply.
The Deepest Thinkers Rarely Went Hungry。
My first assumption was that life was so hard that people went looking for a spiritual way out. But on reflection that doesn't hold together: much of the profound speculation in the Upanishads came from the priestly class, people who never had to worry about food or clothing.
The Buddha is the clearest case of all. He was a prince who could have spent his whole life free of care, and instead he left home to live as an ascetic.
For people like this, the real despair wasn't that life was hard. It was the discovery that even with fine clothes and rich food, there's no escaping birth, old age, sickness and death. That anxiety about life itself drives people inward more forcefully than any material want.
A Metaphysical Revolt, and a Social One。
The dominant religion in India at the time was Brahmanism. It sorted people by birth into four castes, put the power to perform sacrifices and interpret scripture in the hands of the Brahmin priests, and left those at the bottom pinned there from the day they were born.
The Buddha denied that birth decides everything and handed the right to spiritual practice back to everyone. Seen this way, Buddhism was both a new account of the cosmos and of human life and a challenge to a rigid hierarchy. I don't think you can understand it without both halves.
What You Escape Is Suffering, Not Necessarily Rebirth。
Buddhism describes six realms of rebirth: gods, humans, asuras, animals, hungry ghosts and the hells. The usual account is that the ultimate goal of practice is to break out of the cycle altogether.
I prefer to read samsara as a description of suffering. Going round and round, wanting, getting, losing and wanting again, is itself the wheel. What we really want to escape is the suffering.
I should be clear that this is my own reading. In early Buddhist teaching, rebirth was understood as something that literally happens, not as a metaphor.
Wanting Eternal Happiness Is Itself a Craving。
Follow that thought far enough and you run into an interesting paradox. If the point of practice is to be free of suffering forever, which is to say to gain eternal happiness, isn't that the most refined form of greed there is?
Buddhism does in fact draw a line here. The desire that breeds attachment is tanha, "thirst" or craving, and it's the root of suffering. The wish for liberation is a wholesome kind of desire, chanda, and it's treated as a positive force. Nirvana, for its part, isn't a permanent state of bliss. The word literally means "blowing out": the machinery that produces suffering comes to a stop.
So "no longer suffering" and "always being happy" are two different things. Clinging to "I want to be happy forever" is still craving.
What Calm Is Not。
This state is hard to describe directly, so let me start with what it isn't.
It isn't a stagnant pond. Someone once offered an image I like: you sit by the sea listening to the surf. The waves are still breaking and the roar hasn't stopped, but inside you are calm. The world rises and falls as it always has, and you feel all of it, but it no longer pulls you along.
It also made me think of the "heat death" of physics. If heat were spread perfectly evenly and entropy stopped increasing, nothing would ever change again, and even time would lose its meaning. The calm Buddhism talks about is clearly not that kind of uniformity. It's closer to an inner strength and clarity. I can't quite put it into words, but I can sense which way it lies.
How Is This Different from "Lying Flat"?
This is the question I most want to settle. In China there's a popular phrase for opting out of the rat race: tangping, "lying flat."。
Lying flat means giving up on action. This calm means you still do the work and still carry your share, and simply let go of the insistence that things must turn out a certain way. There's an old Chinese saying, "Do what is good, and don't ask what lies ahead," and I think that's roughly the idea.
To be honest, after reading enough of this sort of thing, it starts to feel a little like going around in verbal circles. But if I can understand even part of it, that's enough for me.
Next time, down to the banks of the Nile: the Egyptians were after a kind of balance too, but they took an entirely different road to it.
