Who follows Lagarde? Changing the ECB's guard in a hiking cycle.
The short answer: Spain's Pablo Hernández de Cos will most likely be the next president of the European Central Bank, probably appointed at the EU summit on the seventeenth and eighteenth of December. But this is not a simple two-way race. France and Germany are dividing up three seats.
Why the sudden change? On the twenty-ninth of October, Christine Lagarde announced she would step down on the seventh of February 2027, about nine months early. Her reason was blunt: central bankers must stay neutral, and she cannot remain neutral. She wants freedom of expression. Many link this to France's presidential election next spring; she says she is not a candidate for anything.
Two more board members are leaving too. Isabel Schnabel of Germany, who runs market operations, leaves on the third of January for the IMF. Chief economist Philip Lane of Ireland reaches the end of his term in May. Three of six board members gone in five months, and the Eurogroup president has said the posts will probably be settled as a package.
The two candidates. De Cos was governor of the Bank of Spain from 2018 to 2024 and has run the Bank for International Settlements since July 2025. He is moderate and a consensus builder, and would be the first Spaniard to lead the ECB. Klaas Knot ran the Dutch central bank from 2011 to 2025 and was long a hawkish voice on the Governing Council. De Cos is friendlier to fiscally stretched southern Europe; Knot puts more weight on fighting inflation.
The real table is a Franco-German deal. According to El País and Handelsblatt, the presidency would go to de Cos, backed by France. The chief economist post would go to Germany, with Chancellor Merz's office favouring Princeton economist Markus Brunnermeier, the first German in that job since 2011. Schnabel's market-operations seat would go to France. If Knot won instead, France would get chief economist and Germany would keep market operations. Either way, both deputy seats probably go to Paris and Berlin. Merz said in Dublin on the thirteenth of November that a deal was in sight but not final.
What does the new president inherit? Inflation hit 4.0 percent in October, twice the target, driven by Middle East energy prices; excluding energy it was 2.3 percent. The deposit rate is 2.5 percent, and most economists expect another hike on the seventeenth of December. Third-quarter growth was 0.4 percent, but about half of that came from Ireland. Excluding Ireland, it was 0.2 percent. France shrank by 0.1 percent, Italy was flat, and Spain grew 0.6 percent. The first question for the new president will likely be when to stop hiking, and markets would probably read de Cos as a signal of stopping sooner.
My calls. EU leaders appoint the new president at the December summit: 70 percent. The new president is de Cos: 70 percent, up from 60 on the board. Germany and France each get one of the three vacant seats: 80 percent. Brunnermeier is named chief economist: 45 percent. The ECB hikes by a quarter point on the seventeenth of December: 85 percent.
