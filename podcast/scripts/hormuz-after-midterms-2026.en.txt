A Deadline, Not a Switch: The Strait of Hormuz After the Midterms.
This follows on from After the Blue Wave. Once Democrats won both chambers of Congress, markets immediately bet on a deal, and Brent crude fell below one hundred dollars on November fourth. But ships in the strait are still being attacked. So what has the election actually changed?
The short answer: the election makes a reopening of the strait more likely, but the stretch between now and January, when the new Congress is sworn in, is actually the riskiest window. The most likely path is another round of strikes or a continued standoff first, followed by a limited deal brokered by Qatar.
Part one. Where things stand, as of November fifth.
The strait is still far from normal. About two hundred and fifty days have passed since the war began on February twenty-eighth. Commercial traffic visible on public ship-tracking data is about a tenth of pre-war levels, with a seven-day average of eight point six ships a day according to hormuz dot now. Separately, convoys escorted by the US Navy averaged about thirty-one and a half ships a day from November first to fourth, according to UANI. Different trackers count very differently.
The attacks have not stopped. Reuters, citing the UK Maritime Trade Operations centre, reports that at least five ships have reported attacks since November first, a pace close to October's record of thirty-five. Iran says several more tankers struck mines and caught fire on November fifth.
Iran is letting some ships through. Iranian parliamentary officials say ships from India, China, Iraq and Qatar are exempt from the transit restrictions.
Oil prices have already bet on a deal. On November fifth, Brent traded at about ninety-three to ninety-six dollars, down more than twenty dollars from last week, but still well above the pre-war level of about seventy-two dollars.
Part two. What the election changed.
One, the war's political cost now has a price. Vice President JD Vance said bluntly that the reason we lost is the war, and Trump called that basically true. Republicans have begun distancing themselves from the war, and potential 2028 candidates all want to shed it.
Two, Democrats now hold the purse strings. Senate Democratic leader Chuck Schumer said this war must end. Once the new Congress takes office, Democrats will control the appropriations committees in both chambers, and can cut war funding, issue subpoenas and push war powers resolutions.
Three, but the brakes are limited. Neither chamber comes close to the two-thirds needed to override a veto, and the president can still use force in the name of self-defence. Current government funding expires on December eleventh, and Republicans are also considering folding war funding into a year-end reconciliation bill.
Four, Iran reads it as a win. Tehran interprets the election as a sign that Americans oppose the war, and analysts worry it will raise its price and wait for a constrained president to give ground. Iran's seven conditions include lifting the naval blockade, easing sanctions, guarantees against further strikes, and a regional ceasefire that includes Lebanon. If they are met, Iran says it will reopen the strait within two days.
The core tension: Congress will raise the cost of a long war, but it cannot stop a short, sharp strike. That makes the period from now to early January the window in which Trump has the most freedom to act, and the least constraint from voters.
Part three. Four possible paths, in order of likelihood.
One, a standoff, then a limited deal, the most likely path. The blockade and escorts continue, with occasional self-defence strikes, while Qatar and Oman push forward a narrow arrangement similar to June's memorandum of understanding: lifting the blockade and easing some sanctions in exchange for reopening the strait, with the nuclear question put off. Markets are currently pricing this scenario. The risk is that June's memorandum collapsed precisely at the hands of hardliners on both sides.
Two, one hard blow, then an exit. Before the election there were reports that Washington had given allies advance notice. After the election, there could be a short, intense strike, for example on the Pickaxe Mountain site near Natanz, followed by a declaration of victory and talks on reopening the strait. That would bring a spike in oil prices before any deal. Iran has warned that any strike would amount to restarting the full war.
Three, a full return to war, the tail risk. Israel is pushing for it, and three aircraft carriers are already in the region. If strikes hit energy facilities on Kharg Island, Iran retaliates against Gulf energy infrastructure and undersea cables, and the Houthis escalate in the Red Sea, the strait would stay closed for longer and oil would return to high levels. Politically, this is the worst option for Trump.
Four, a comprehensive peace deal, unlikely in the short term. Washington wants a verifiable commitment to give up nuclear weapons, while Iran wants its conditions met before any nuclear talks. The gap is too wide.
Part four. What it means for Europe.
The strait is the main source of the eurozone's energy inflation, with the energy component at twenty point eight percent in October. If the first scenario plays out, inflation could peak over the winter, giving the European Central Bank reason to pause after December. The second and third scenarios would push the ECB into a deeper stagflation dilemma.
Iran's selective passage for countries such as China and India means that even if the strait partly reopens, transit and insurance costs for European ships may recover more slowly.
Part five. Dates to watch.
During November: whether the US launches a new round of strikes, and progress in Qatar's mediation.
November twenty-ninth: the OPEC Plus meeting sets production policy for 2027.
December eleventh: US government funding expires, the first head-on clash over war funding.
December seventeenth: the ECB's rate decision.
Early January 2027: the new Congress takes office, and Democrats take over appropriations and investigative powers.
In one sentence: the election changed the war's deadline, not Trump's switch. The strait is more likely to reopen in earnest in the first half of next year than in the coming weeks, and oil prices will swing until then.
This analysis is reasoning based on public reports, not a forecast, and not investment advice.
