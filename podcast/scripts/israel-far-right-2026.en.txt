Is Israel's far-right era about to end?
Here is the short answer. The far right's era in government is probably ending. Its era of shaping the agenda is not.
On October 27th, Israel elected its 26th Knesset, 120 seats, with turnout close to 78 percent. Netanyahu's bloc won 53 seats: Likud 27, Ben-Gvir's Otzma Yehudit 7, Smotrich's Religious Zionism 6, and the two ultra-Orthodox parties 13 between them. Gadi Eisenkot's bloc won 48. And two Arab-led parties won a record 19. Nobody reached the 61 needed for a majority.
Here's the interesting part. The far-right vote did not collapse. Their two parties hold 13 seats, about the same as last time. They lost the coalition arithmetic, not their voters. What really changed the picture was a surge in Arab turnout.
On November 8th, President Herzog gave the mandate to Eisenkot, a former army chief. He has 28 days, plus up to 14 more, so until around December 6th, and at the latest around December 20th. The catch is that the 67 MKs who recommended him are not 67 MKs willing to govern together. Liberman won't rely on Arab parties, and Bennett has moved further right since 2021. The most realistic route is to bring in Shas and United Torah Judaism. 48 plus 13 is exactly 61. The price is a compromise on military service for the ultra-Orthodox.
So would a new government be different? At home, yes: an inquiry into October 7th, an end to the judicial overhaul, a budget. But on the Palestinians, the gap is much smaller than the headline suggests. Eisenkot's platform does not clearly back a Palestinian state, Bennett wants troops on Gaza's Yellow Line, and Liberman wants to annex Area C. So Israel would move from a far-right government to a right-leaning centrist one. The tone changes. The steering wheel doesn't turn around.
Now my forecasts, each with a probability, so you can come back and check.
One. Eisenkot becomes prime minister by the end of January 2027: 60 percent, most likely with the ultra-Orthodox on board. Talks fail and Israel votes again next spring: 30 percent. Netanyahu forms a new government: 10 percent. The signal to watch is whether United Torah Judaism signs a draft compromise by mid-December.
Two. Neither Ben-Gvir nor Smotrich is a minister by June 2027: 75 percent. But together they still win at least 10 seats next time: 70 percent. Being out of power will make them more radical, not make them disappear.
Three, Gaza. The army still controls most of Gaza, around the Yellow Line, at the end of 2027: 80 percent. The international stabilization force deploys to at least one area by mid-2027: 55 percent. Hamas fully and verifiably disarms by the end of 2027: just 10 percent. Reconstruction will start in pilot zones, not across the Strip.
Four, the West Bank. Formal annexation legislation in 2027: under 10 percent. But settler violence and settlement growth don't fall noticeably: 70 percent.
Five. Saudi Arabia and Israel announce normalization by the end of 2027: 20 percent. An Israeli government accepts the principle of a Palestinian state: under 5 percent.
In one line: the far right will leave government, but not Israeli politics. The forecast I'm most confident in is that, whoever governs, Gaza's word for 2027 will be limited international administration. Not peace, and not all-out war. What could make me wrong across the board is a major attack, or a renewed war with Iran, during coalition talks. That would push the whole political spectrum right once again.
This is personal reasoning based on public reporting, and the probabilities are subjective. Let's come back in a year and check.
