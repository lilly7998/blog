After the Blue Wave: The US Midterm Results and What Comes Next for Europe's Economy.
In our November first piece, A Restless Autumn, we named the US midterm elections and the eurozone's October flash inflation figure as the two variables most worth watching. Now both have answers: Democrats have won both chambers of Congress, and eurozone inflation has risen to four percent. This piece follows on from that one.
Part one. The results: a bigger blue wave than most forecasts expected.
The Senate. Before the election, Republicans held a fifty-three to forty-seven majority, and Democrats needed a net gain of four seats. According to CBS News projections as of the evening of November fourth, Eastern Time, Democrats have secured at least fifty-four seats. They flipped seven Republican-held seats, in North Carolina, South Carolina, Texas, Kansas, Iowa, Ohio and Maine, and held Georgia, New Hampshire and Michigan. In Texas, Talarico beat Paxton; in Maine, Jackson defeated five-term senator Susan Collins; and Kansas elected a Democratic senator for the first time in nearly a century.
Still undecided. At the time of writing, two seats remained uncalled: Alaska, Sullivan against Peltola, and Mississippi, Hyde-Smith against Collom. CBS rates both as toss-ups. In Mississippi the two candidates are only a few dozen votes apart, with about fifteen thousand provisional ballots still to be counted. Neither seat affects control of the chamber; they only decide whether Democrats end up with fifty-four, fifty-five or fifty-six seats.
The House. Before the election, Republicans held two hundred and nineteen seats and Democrats two hundred and fourteen. CBS News projected on the night of November third that Democrats would retake the House. According to Euronews, as of five past ten on November fourth, Central European Time, Democrats had won two hundred and twenty-eight seats, above the majority line of two hundred and eighteen, against one hundred and thirty-nine for Republicans, with dozens of races still open. Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries expects a final tally of at least two hundred and fifty-five. Speaker Mike Johnson has said he will not run for minority leader.
According to CBS exit polls, Trump's approval rating is around thirty-eight percent, and eighty-nine percent of voters said the cost of living was an important factor in their vote. The new Congress takes office in January 2027; until then, Republicans keep their majorities in both chambers.
Part two. Eurozone inflation in October: four percent, driven by energy.
Eurostat's flash estimate, published on November fourth, puts headline inflation at four point zero percent in October, up from three point eight percent in September. Core inflation, which excludes energy, food, alcohol and tobacco, held at two point five percent. Energy inflation rose to twenty point eight percent from eighteen point nine percent, and services inflation edged up to three point three percent from three point two percent. Prices rose zero point four percent on the month.
This is the first time inflation has reached four percent since September 2023, in line with market expectations. The gaps between countries are wide: Italy and Greece at five point six percent, Belgium at five point two, Spain at four point nine, France at three point eight, and Germany at two point eight, where inflation even eased slightly on the month. Full data will be published on November eighteenth.
The split between headline and core is worth noting. Headline inflation keeps climbing, but core inflation is steady at two point five percent. That suggests the pressure comes mainly from energy prices pushed up by the Iran war, and has not yet spread clearly into wages and services.
Part three. What it means for Europe.
One, tariffs and trade: a change in Congress does not mean tariffs go away. According to Euronews, after the Supreme Court struck down the emergency tariffs in February, the administration moved them under Section 301 of the Trade Act, which the president can use without a vote in Congress. Democrats can hold hearings and delay nominations, but they will find it hard to take back this power. ING economists even argue that a president constrained at home may lean more on unilateral trade tools, and new tariffs on Europe cannot be ruled out. A survey cited by the EU Institute for Security Studies found that only thirty-seven percent of Democratic voters think tariffs on the EU should be removed. The fifteen percent baseline tariff framework is very likely to stay in place in the short term.
Two, Ukraine and sanctions on Russia: an extra brake, but the money may not arrive. Democrats broadly support aid to Ukraine, are already challenging Trump's decision to allow imports of Russian diesel, and have pledged to oppose troop withdrawals from Europe. But scholars at the European Council on Foreign Relations warn that over the past two years, the White House has let funds approved by Congress expire by delaying their release. In other words, money that Congress writes into law for Ukraine will not necessarily be spent. The EU is working to fill Ukraine's funding gap of about seventy billion euros next year. Many analysts also worry that a president frustrated at home will turn to foreign policy as an outlet, which could actually raise uncertainty for Europe.
Three, the European Central Bank: about a seventy percent chance of a hike in December. The ECB's deposit rate is currently two point five percent. According to CNBC, after the data came out, markets priced the probability of a hike at the December seventeenth meeting at more than seventy-two percent. However, turmoil in the French bond market is seen as a constraint: if borrowing costs rise further, the ECB will be more cautious about raising rates.
Four, the euro and markets: risk appetite returns. On November fourth, the STOXX 600 index closed up one point eight two percent at six hundred and fifty-seven point four, its highest since August twenty-sixth. Banks led with gains of about four point six percent, clean energy stocks rose and defence stocks fell. The dollar weakened, and the euro rose about half a percent to around one point one two four seven dollars. Germany's ten-year bond yield was broadly flat near three point three three percent, and Brent crude fell about eight percent on the day. Most analysts think the election's effect on currencies may be short-lived, and that the dollar will still be driven mainly by the Federal Reserve.
Part four. Updated scenarios.
What follows is a judgement based on current information, not a forecast.
One, the base case, which is more likely: slow rate hikes under stagflationary pressure. Headline inflation hovers around four percent while core stays stable, and the ECB raises rates by a quarter point in December before pausing to watch. Growth stays weak, borrowing costs in southern Europe remain under pressure, and France's bond spread is still the barometer of sentiment. The EU–US tariff framework stays broadly the same.
Two, the easing case: falling energy prices let inflation peak. If tensions with Iran ease and oil extends its November fourth slide, inflation could peak over the winter, giving the ECB reason to pause after December and supporting the euro and European stocks.
Three, the tension case: a foreign-policy outlet brings new shocks. If the White House turns to more aggressive unilateral moves on tariffs, Russia policy or troop levels in Europe, or if conflict in the Gulf escalates and pushes up gas prices, Europe would face higher inflation and weaker growth at the same time, deepening the ECB's dilemma.
Compared with our November first view, the biggest change is that Congress's check on the administration has clearly strengthened, but on trade and foreign policy, the two lines that matter most for Europe, the president's executive power is largely unchanged. For Europe, reducing dependence on any single partner and speeding up defence and energy autonomy remain the surer path.
The next milestones to watch: the final counts in Alaska and Mississippi, full eurozone inflation data on November eighteenth, the November flash estimate on December first, and the ECB's rate meeting on December seventeenth.
