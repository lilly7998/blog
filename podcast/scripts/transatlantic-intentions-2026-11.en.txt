Everyone Has an Angle: Intentions and Next Moves in Transatlantic Politics and Economics.
After the Blue Wave covered the results of the US midterms. This piece takes the next step. Everyone can see the events and the data. The two questions I want to answer are: what does each player actually want, and how will things unfold over the next two months?
The short version: the central banks on both sides of the Atlantic are following the script. The real uncertainty lies in two budgets: the EU's long-term budget for 2028–2034 and France's budget for 2027. Both reach their decision points around December 17, and that week carries the most risk.
All probabilities here are my own judgments, not market pricing. In mid-December, I will check them against what actually happened.
1. The White House: it lost the election, so why double down on tariffs?
A week after the midterms, White House trade adviser Peter Navarro said the administration is "quadrupling down on tariffs." On the same day, a Reuters/Ipsos poll found that 62% of Americans support repealing them.
Intentions. I see three layers:
Tariffs are one of the few tools the president can use without Congress. With both chambers gone, trade and foreign policy are where Trump still holds the initiative. Backing down now would amount to admitting the voters were right.
Shifting the blame for high prices. Onto the Federal Reserve, and onto Congress. If Democrats push to repeal the tariffs, he can veto the bill and accuse them of being "soft on China.".
Building leverage for China talks. Gabriel Felbermayr of the German Council of Economic Experts notes that several deadlines on China tariffs expire early next year. Escalating now is a way to stockpile bargaining chips.
Forecasts.
After Democrats take over Congress in January, they will vote to limit tariffs, but overriding a veto takes a two-thirds majority, and they don't have it. The main tariffs are still in place at the end of 2027: about 80%.
Democrats will put their energy into investigations: tariffs, the Iran war, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth. That lays groundwork for 2028; it does little to bring prices down.
On China, expect either a modest escalation or another extension in the first quarter of next year. German exports to the US would be collateral damage.
2. Warsh: Trump's pick, so why is he raising rates?
October CPI rose 0.4% month on month, above the 0.3% expected. Annual inflation was 3.7% in September. The US 10-year Treasury yield briefly reached 4.99%. Fed Chair Kevin Warsh was Trump's choice, yet he keeps raising rates.
Intentions.
What he fears most is the label of "Trump's sock puppet," which is exactly what Senator Elizabeth Warren calls him. With inflation this high and long-term yields near 5%, holding rates would get him punished immediately by the bond market.
With Democrats controlling the Senate, the Fed's independence is actually better protected. Warren is set to chair the Senate Banking Committee next year, and Warsh needs to establish his hawkish credentials before he sits in front of her.
Forecasts.
A 25 basis point hike on December 9: about 85%; 50 basis points: about 10%. Another hike in January: about 55%.
Trump will most likely criticize Warsh by name after the December hike. But with the Senate in Democratic hands, there is no realistic way to replace him.
3. Merz: "No Ukraine funding, no budget." What does he really want?
In a letter to European Council President António Costa, Friedrich Merz said he will not discuss the EU's 2028–2034 long-term budget until every member state commits to financing Ukraine. Slovak Prime Minister Robert Fico called it "blackmail" on the spot. Hungary, Slovakia, Czechia, and now Bulgaria, which elected a Moscow-friendly prime minister in April, all refuse to join the next round of loans for Ukraine. Kyiv faces a funding gap of about €70 billion, and the EU has pledged €45 billion.
Intentions.
On the surface it is about Ukraine. In practice, Ukraine is leverage for a smaller budget. Germany is the largest net contributor, and its own economic advisers have just warned that defence and pension spending are pushing deficits out of control. Berlin wants the countries that take the most from the EU and give the least to Ukraine to pay a price.
Changing the rules. Berlin wants Ukraine funding decisions moved from unanimity to qualified majority voting, which would strip those four countries of their veto.
A story for voters at home. "Germany no longer pays alone" is a message for German voters, and a way to squeeze the far-right AfD.
Forecasts.
The informal summit in Dublin on November 13 will produce statements, not decisions.
The €45 billion is pieced together by year-end without those four countries: about 65%.
A deal on the seven-year budget at the special summit on November 26–27: about 15%; by year-end: about 25%. More likely it slips into 2027, where it collides with elections in France, Poland and Italy.
Qualified majority voting on Ukraine funding in place within a year: under 10%.
4. The ECB: three seats, one package.
Christine Lagarde announced on October 29 that she will leave early, on February 7, 2027. The candidates to succeed her are former Dutch central bank governor Klaas Knot and Spain's Pablo Hernández de Cos, now head of the Bank for International Settlements. The Eurogroup wants a decision by year-end. Isabel Schnabel, the board member in charge of market operations, leaves on January 3, 2027, and chief economist Philip Lane's term ends in May 2027, so all three seats will probably be traded as a package among France, Germany, Spain and the Netherlands.
Intentions.
France backs Hernández de Cos to protect itself. Its spreads are widening, and both Moody's and Fitch have moved their outlooks to negative. Paris wants a president willing to steady the bond market when it counts. Knot is known as a hawk.
Germany may care less about the presidency than about Schnabel's market operations seat or the chief economist job. A likely outcome: a southerner as president, northerners in the seats with real power.
Lane is drawing a line for France. He said publicly that the ECB should not use its tools to help France with its budget, and that "this needs to be fixed in Paris." He said it just before the French budget fight peaks, and the timing is not an accident.
Forecasts.
Hernández de Cos gets the job: about 60%; Knot: about 35%. A decision by year-end: about 60%.
The ECB raises rates by 25 basis points on December 17, taking the deposit rate to 2.75%: about 85%. After that, the pace depends on whether energy prices keep falling.
5. France: the link most likely to break.
On November 4, the National Assembly rejected the revenue part of the 2027 budget by 458 votes to 0, a first under the Fifth Republic. The bill went to the Senate in the government's original form, with debate from November 20 to December 9, then a joint committee, and a deadline at midnight on December 17. Last year, Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu bought the Socialists' tolerance by suspending the pension reform. This year there is no such deal yet.
Intentions.
With the 2027 presidential election approaching, no party wants to own €43 billion in spending cuts. A vote of zero is a statement, not legislation.
Lecornu is performing for bond markets and rating agencies. Whatever parliament does, he has to defend the 5% deficit target for 2027.
Forecasts (to the end of January 2027):
France muddles through on a special law or ordinances, with no budget formally passed: about 45%.
The government forces the budget through with Article 49.3 and survives the no-confidence vote: about 30%.
The government falls: about 25%.
If it falls, the French-German spread will likely jump from 146 basis points to above 170. But as long as the market stays orderly, the ECB will not step in.
6. Connecting the threads.
Inflation is punishing incumbents. The US midterms were lost on prices. In Europe, from the French parliament to populist governments in Central Europe, "we won't pay for others" is the most effective slogan around.
Both central banks may overtighten. The energy shock from the Iran war is fading; Brent fell from nearly $118 to $94. If rate hikes continue into the first half of 2027, Germany, which just had its growth forecast raised, and France, with its strained public finances, will be hurt first.
Europe is redrawing its camps with money. Support for Ukraine is shifting from an EU consensus to a coalition of the willing. A two-speed Europe is taking shape.
