A Restless Autumn: Europe's Politics and Economy in 2026.
This autumn, Europe's streets have been unusually busy. From Paris and Milan to Madrid, Lisbon and Tirana, the cost of living, housing and public services have become the common themes of protest. At the same time, a dense cycle of elections is under way. This piece tries to map out the recent situation and offer a balanced view of where the economy may head over the next few months.
Part one. A wave of protests: different starting points, similar demands.
France is the centre of this wave. The movement began in mid-September, when high-school students demanded better school buildings and more teachers. Unions and public-sector workers then joined, and the demands widened to the cost of living and public spending. On October seventeenth, marches turned into rioting on the edges of some cities, and by late October around two thousand four hundred people had been arrested. On October thirty-first, France saw a third weekend in a row of demonstrations numbering in the hundreds of thousands. They were largely peaceful, though there were scattered clashes in Paris and elsewhere. The government has made some concessions, which student groups say fall far short.
In Spain and Portugal, the focus is housing. Spanish house prices rose about eighty percent between 2015 and 2025, while wages rose only about thirty percent. A tent camp has appeared in Madrid's Puerta del Sol. Housing activists plan to hold Spain's first general strike in fifteen years on November eleventh.
In northern Italy, students are protesting low wages and the cost of living, while in the south, truck drivers and farmers have blocked motorway exits over fuel prices. The Meloni government has sent five hundred soldiers to Milan to help keep order. Belgium, Greece and Albania have also seen protests of varying size, and Germany has had small protests over education funding.
There is no single organisation behind these movements, but social media carries slogans and tactics across borders. Most observers see the same underlying cause: a younger generation's disappointment over housing, incomes and public services.
Part two. Elections: a dense political cycle.
Serbia, October twenty-fifth. The Student List, which grew out of the student movement, won the parliamentary election, taking 141 of 250 seats according to Ipsos and CeSID projections. Aleksandar Vučić, in power for many years, conceded defeat. Turnout was close to seventy percent, the highest since 1990. Hungary had already seen a long-serving leader leave office.
Spain, November twenty-ninth. Pedro Sánchez called an early election after his housing bill failed in parliament. Most polls suggest the People's Party could form a coalition with the far-right Vox, but the outcome remains uncertain.
France, the 2027 presidential election. Marine Le Pen continues to lead the polls, and her law-and-order message is seen as likely to benefit from the unrest. However, the first poll after the riots showed little change in voting intentions for now.
Poland. Tension between Donald Tusk's governing coalition and the opposition Law and Justice party has sharpened over the suspension of the head of the Constitutional Tribunal, about a year before the next parliamentary election.
Italy and Greece also face important elections in the period ahead.
Part three. The economy: slowing growth, rising inflation, tight interest rates.
A few recent figures sketch the eurozone's position. Third-quarter GDP grew zero point four percent on the previous quarter in the preliminary estimate, above the expected zero point two percent, after zero point six percent in the second quarter. Germany grew zero point two percent, France shrank zero point one percent, Italy was flat, and Spain grew zero point six percent. September inflation was three point eight percent, up from three point two percent in August and a three-year high. The European Central Bank's deposit rate stands at two point five percent; it held rates on October twenty-ninth, after raising them by a quarter point in both June and September. Government debt was eighty-eight point eight percent of GDP at the end of the second quarter, and consumer confidence in October was minus seventeen point nine.
Energy is the main driver of inflation. The conflict between the United States and Iran has pushed up oil and gas prices, while the energy subsidies that governments set up for households are expiring one after another. In its October statement, the ECB said the energy shock creates upside risks to inflation and downside risks to growth. So far, core inflation and wages show no clear second-round effects, but financing conditions have tightened. Rising borrowing costs in France, Italy, Spain and Greece have narrowed the fiscal room governments have to respond to the protesters' demands.
Part four. The US midterms: how big a variable?
The United States holds its midterm elections on November third. Democrats lead in national generic-ballot polls, and most forecasts expect them to retake the House, while the Senate remains close. Donald Trump's average approval rating is around thirty-eight percent.
For Europe, the impact may be more limited than it seems. Under the earlier EU–US trade deal, most EU goods face a fifteen percent US tariff. Even if Democrats control Congress, the president keeps broad powers over tariffs and foreign policy, and can veto legislation. EU and NATO officials generally expect the unpredictability of US policy not to change fundamentally because of the election. Still, the result will shape US support for Ukraine, sanctions on Russia and the mood of transatlantic trade talks, and it will affect European markets through the dollar and risk appetite.
Part five. What next: several possible paths.
What follows is a judgement based on current information, not a firm forecast.
One, the short term, November to December. October's flash inflation figure, due November fourth, is key. If inflation keeps rising, the ECB is fairly likely to raise rates by another quarter point at its December seventeenth meeting, and markets have already partly priced this in. A hike would further raise borrowing costs for southern European countries.
Two, growth. The eurozone will most likely keep growing slowly. France and Italy face the risk of stagnation or even a mild contraction, while Spain and Ireland are relatively strong. Winter energy prices and Germany's low gas storage are the main downside risks.
Three, the interplay of politics and public finances. Protests and elections may push governments to spend more on social programmes, but high interest rates and high debt limit how far they can go. France's public finances are under particular scrutiny, and its bond spread may become a barometer of market sentiment.
Four, the American factor. If Democrats win control of Congress, domestic checks on tariffs may grow stronger, but the EU–US tariff framework is unlikely to change much in the short term. If Republicans hold both chambers, trade policy will be more continuous. Either way, Europe is likely to keep diversifying its defence and trade partners, for example by strengthening ties with Canada.
Overall, Europe is in a phase where three pressures overlap: an energy shock, fiscal constraints and an election cycle. Over the next few months, inflation data, the ECB's December decision, Spain's election and the US midterm results will together decide whether this restlessness gradually fades, or carries on into France's 2027 presidential election.
