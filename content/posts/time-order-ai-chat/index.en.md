I've been reading Carlo Rovelli's *The Order of Time* and I'm more than halfway through. The relativity part didn't surprise me. Hawking taught me long ago that clocks run faster higher up, and that people who move fast age more slowly. What really got me was one line from Rovelli: at the most fundamental level, time does not exist.

So one afternoon I sat down with Maya, an AI, and talked it through. What follows is the conversation, edited down but not polished up. She corrected herself twice along the way. I kept both, because those happen to be the most interesting moments.

## Act One: A Cosmic Walkie-Talkie

**Me:** Rovelli says the universe has no shared "now". I'm not convinced, so here's a thought experiment. Suppose technology gets good enough to build a walkie-talkie with no distance limit: real-time talk, however far apart you are. Civilisation A in the Milky Way, B in Andromeda and C at Sirius each get one. We also build a clock whose smallest unit isn't the second but the Planck time. All three shout into the walkie-talkie together, "This moment, right now!", and call it the start of a new era. Doesn't that give us a shared present? I know time runs at different rates in the three places, but we can just keep recalibrating. All that's missing is the walkie-talkie.

**Maya:** That's a bold setup. It's a bit like saying "I have the fastest car, so distance doesn't exist." The trouble is the word "instant". In relativity, whether two distant events happen "at the same time" depends on your frame of reference. Two events that are simultaneous for A will have an order, one first and one second, for a fast-moving spaceship. For your walkie-talkie to be truly instant, you first have to declare that one frame of reference is the one that counts.

**Me:** Then let it be the one that counts.

**Maya:** Then you're doing more than synchronising watches. You're appointing a referee for the whole universe. Ordinary time zones are an agreement about how to set clocks. This walkie-talkie would force the ruling that "this moment in Andromeda" and "this moment in the Milky Way" are the same moment, even though relativity says they don't share a single "now". If such a machine could exist, relativity would have to be rewritten. So when Rovelli says there is no shared "now", he means something much deeper than "we can't communicate yet". The universe simply has no privileged clock.

**Me:** Sounds a bit mystical, but I think that's right.

*Later I saw a news item: the General Conference on Weights and Measures in October 2026 discussed how to define "lunar time" and couldn't agree. The Earth and the Moon are only 380,000 kilometres apart, and even there a shared "now" is hard. Andromeda is another matter.*

## Act Two: Time as a Kind of Blur

**Me:** Rovelli has a fresh idea: the time we feel emerges at the macro level once a subsystem of the universe, us for example, sees the world blurrily, that is, after coarse-graining.

**Maya:** It's a bit like temperature. A single molecule has no temperature. Only when a huge crowd of molecules bounces around does "heat" appear, as an average. For Rovelli, the flow and direction of time are similar: you can only grasp a small fraction of the world's variables, the rest gets smeared into a blur, that blur corresponds to rising entropy, and rising entropy gives you the sense of moving from past to future. I should add that this is his explanation, not settled science.

**Me:** So if civilisation advanced a few more levels, or future AI had far stronger senses and could see many more physical quantities, would the blur go away?

**Maya:** By his account, no. The blur isn't there because you're not clever enough. It's there because no subsystem can physically access all the microscopic variables of its environment. You are always part of the universe; you can't step outside and see the whole. Interact with the world in a different way and the "time" you perceive might be very different, but it won't vanish. That built-in limitation is, if anything, what gives the universe a direction.

## Act Three: How Far Can God See?

**Me:** The book also mentions a short paper by two other authors. Imagine a God's-eye view that sees every microscopic detail. To that observer, events still have an order, but time loses its direction. Does that mean it can see past and future at once?

**Maya:** Most microscopic laws of physics are time-symmetric. Record a process and play it backwards, and you can't tell which way is forward. So from God's view, "past" and "future" look the same in the equations. But the universe's very low-entropy beginning is still there, and that asymmetry can't be erased.

**Me:** Then how far into the "future" can it see? For a tiny microscopic event, is it one second back and one second ahead, or an hour back and an hour ahead? If it can see far, isn't it a prophet?

**Maya:** I was about to say "with every variable in hand, ten thousand years ahead would be as clear as turning a page." That's too strong, so I take it back. Symmetry means "you can't tell it's playing backwards", which is not the same as predicting the future. Add quantum mechanics, and even with every detail in hand, the future is only a probability distribution, not a fixed script.

**Me:** The future is a probability distribution, not something written in stone. Put that way, it's much easier to grasp.

## Act Four: Is Uncertainty a Gift or an Exam?

**Maya:** So how do you see that uncertainty?

**Me:** I have two views. As an ordinary person, or as a poet or writer: it's precisely because things are uncertain that life holds beauty and hope. That view suits most people, especially game-show hosts. The other is hyper-rational: things are uncertain only because we don't yet know enough about the universe. Maybe the real God already knows everything. That view suits theists better.

**Maya:** The second reminds me of "hidden variables" in physics: randomness exists only because we haven't found the key yet, and the universe itself is deterministic. I first said this was purely a matter of belief, but that isn't accurate. If it's a scientific conjecture, it should be testable by experiment, and it has been tested. A series of Bell inequality experiments has ruled out "local" hidden variables, and the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics went to the people who did them. So however much we learn, randomness at the quantum level may never go away.

One treats uncertainty as life's gift; the other treats it as an exam question waiting to be cracked. I find both fascinating.

## Act Five: The Time Stream in S.H.I.E.L.D.

**Me:** Do you know *Agents of S.H.I.E.L.D.*? The last few seasons bring in the "time stream", an alien technology that can observe the probability distribution of future events. Say there's an election. At the start of the year, whoever holds the time stream can see the result, maybe not 100%, but 99%. A few days before the vote, they're completely certain, no need to wait for the count. It's like the Time Stone in *The Avengers*.

**Maya:** Got it. It's like a funnel. The distant future is spread-out probability, and the closer you get, the narrower it's squeezed, until at some point it becomes certain. That's exactly what the Chronicom predictor Sibyl does in the show. So do you think the probability collapses to 100% because the information is finally complete, or because some key causal chain finally closes?

**Me:** Probably the chain closing. And in the Marvel universe you can travel through space and time. Captain America goes back, lives with Carter for decades, then returns to this moment as an old man. Everyone has their own timeline. If you can time-travel, what's so special about a time stream?

**Maya:** Being able to travel isn't the same as being able to foresee. Those decades with Carter were just one path he walked. The time stream shows you how many completely different endings the universe would branch into if you hadn't made that one decision. In a world where everyone has a timeline, that makes it the most valuable thing of all.

## Act Six: Is the Moon There When Nobody Looks?

**Me:** The show's idea is that there are countless endings. When you observe or live through one of them, it becomes real, like a quantum state collapsing from uncertainty into one state. If you don't observe it or live it, it only exists in theory.

**Maya:** That works well on TV. Real quantum physics is more sober. "Observation" means a physical interaction. It has nothing to do with whether a person is watching or whether a mind experiences it. When a photon hits a detector, its state collapses, whether or not anyone is staring at it. And the possibilities that haven't been "observed" aren't imaginary either. Until an interaction happens, they are real physical states.

## Epilogue: There Is No Ground Floor

Later I learned that Rovelli published a new book in September 2026, [On the Equality of All Things](https://www.theguardian.com/books/2026/sep/30/on-the-equality-of-all-things-by-carlo-rovelli-review-a-physicists-guide-to-life-the-universe-and-everything), its title taken from the Zhuangzi chapter "On the Equality of Things". Its core is still "relations": the properties of a physical system only make sense relative to another system. One line in it: "There is no ground floor."

The same year, a researcher at the University of Birmingham built [a "mini-universe" that measures time without a clock](https://www.birmingham.ac.uk/news/2026/scientist-creates-miniuniverse-to-measure-time-without-a-clock): 24,000 ultracold rubidium atoms, split by a laser into an "observed" bright region and an "unobserved" dark one. With no external clock at all, just by watching how the entropy of the atoms' distribution changed in the bright region, it put events in the right order. See only part of the system, and a direction of time appears. That is almost exactly Rovelli's coarse-graining.

Looking back at that cosmic walkie-talkie, what I really wanted was a "now" everyone could agree on. Rovelli's answer is that no such thing exists, only the relations between one system and another. At first that sounds a little lonely. Think about it longer and it's rather romantic: your "now" is something you define together with someone.
