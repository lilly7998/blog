A follow-up to [After the Blue Wave](/en/posts/us-midterms-europe-2026/). Once Democrats won both chambers of Congress, markets immediately bet on "talks": Brent crude fell below $100 on 4 November. But ships in the strait are still being attacked. What did the election actually change?

**The bottom line**: the election makes a reopening of the strait more likely, but the window from now until the new Congress takes office in January is the riskiest one. The most likely path is another round of strikes or continued stalemate first, then a limited deal mediated by Qatar.

## 1. Where things stand (as of 5 November)

- **The strait is still not back to normal.** About 250 days have passed since the war began on 28 February. Merchant traffic visible on public AIS is about a tenth of pre-war levels (hormuz.now seven-day average: 8.6 ships a day); separately, US-escorted convoys averaged about 31.5 ships a day from 1 to 4 November (UANI). Different counting methods give very different numbers.
- **The attacks have not stopped.** Reuters, citing the UK's UKMTO, says at least five ships have reported attacks since 1 November, a pace close to October's record (35 ships). Iran says several more tankers hit mines and caught fire on 5 November.
- **Iran is letting some ships through.** Iranian parliamentary officials say ships from four countries, India, China, Iraq and Qatar, are exempt from the transit restrictions.
- **Oil has priced in talks early.** On 5 November Brent traded at about $93 to $96, more than $20 below last week but still far above the pre-war level of about $72.

## 2. What the election changed

1. **The war's political cost has been priced.** Vice President Vance said bluntly, "The reason we lost is the war," and Trump called that "basically true". Republicans have started distancing themselves from the war, and every potential 2028 candidate wants to drop the burden.
2. **Democrats get the purse strings.** Senate Democratic leader Schumer said "this war has to end". Once the new Congress is sworn in, Democrats will run both appropriations committees and can cut war funding, issue subpoenas and push war-powers resolutions.
3. **But the brakes are limited.** Neither chamber is anywhere near the two-thirds needed to override a veto, and the president can still use force in the name of "self-defence". Current government funding expires on 11 December, and Republicans are also considering folding war funding into a year-end reconciliation bill.
4. **Iran reads it as "we won".** Tehran sees the election as American public opinion turning against the war, and analysts worry it will raise its price and wait for a constrained president to give ground. Iran's "seven conditions" include lifting the naval blockade, easing sanctions, guarantees that strikes will stop, and a regional ceasefire that includes Lebanon; once they are met, it says it will reopen the strait within two days.

**The core tension**: Congress can raise the cost of a long war, but it cannot stop a short, hard strike. That makes the period from now to early January the window in which Trump has the most freedom to act and is least constrained by voters.

## 3. Four paths (from most to least likely)

1. **Stalemate, then a limited deal (most likely).** The blockade and escorts continue, with occasional "defensive" strikes, while mediation by Qatar and Oman moves toward a narrow arrangement like June's memorandum of understanding: lifting the blockade plus some sanctions relief in exchange for reopening the strait, with the nuclear question put off. Markets are currently priced for this. The risk is that the June memorandum collapsed precisely because of hardliners on both sides.
2. **"One hard punch, then exit."** Before the election there were reports that the US had briefed allies, and a short, intense strike may follow (for example on "Pickaxe Mountain" near Natanz), after which Washington declares victory and negotiates the strait's reopening. This would bring a spike in oil prices before any deal. Iran has warned that any strike would amount to restarting the war in full.
3. **Full resumption of the war (tail risk).** Israel is pushing for it, and three aircraft carriers are already in the region. If strikes hit energy facilities on Kharg Island, Iran retaliates against Gulf energy sites and undersea cables, and the Houthis escalate in the Red Sea, the strait would stay closed longer and oil would return to high levels. Politically, this is the worst deal for Trump.
4. **A full peace agreement (unlikely in the short term).** The US wants a verifiable commitment to give up nuclear weapons; Iran wants its conditions met before nuclear talks. The gap is too wide.

## 4. What it means for Europe

- The strait is the main source of eurozone energy inflation (October's energy component: 20.8%). If path 1 happens, inflation could peak this winter and the ECB would have reason to pause after December; paths 2 and 3 would push the ECB into a deeper stagflation dilemma.
- Iran's "selective passage" for China, India and others means that even if the strait partly reopens, transit and insurance costs for European ships may recover more slowly.

## 5. Dates to watch

- **During November**: whether the US launches a new round of strikes; progress in Qatar's mediation
- **29 November**: OPEC+ meeting on 2027 production policy
- **11 December**: US government funding expires, the first head-on fight over war funding
- **17 December**: ECB rate decision
- **Early January 2027**: the new Congress is sworn in and Democrats take over appropriations and investigative powers

**In one sentence**: the election changed the war's "deadline", not Trump's "switch". The strait is more likely to reopen in earnest in the first half of next year than in the coming weeks, and oil prices will swing until then.
