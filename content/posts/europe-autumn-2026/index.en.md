Europe's streets have been unusually busy this autumn. From Paris and Milan to Madrid, Lisbon and Tirana, the cost of living, housing and public services have become common themes of protest, while a dense cycle of elections is under way. This post tries to map the recent situation and give a reasonably neutral view of where the economy may go over the next few months.

## 1. A wave of protest: different starts, similar demands

**France** is at the centre of this wave. The movement began in mid-September with secondary-school students demanding better buildings and more teachers. Unions and public-sector workers then joined, and the demands widened to the cost of living and public spending. The 17 October marches turned into riots on the edges of some cities, and by late October about 2,400 people had been arrested. On 31 October France saw its third weekend in a row of demonstrations hundreds of thousands strong. They were mostly peaceful, though there were scattered clashes in Paris and elsewhere. The government has made some concessions; student groups say they are far from enough.

In **Spain and Portugal** the focus is housing. Spanish house prices rose by about 80% between 2015 and 2025, while wages rose only about 30%; a tent camp appeared in Madrid's Puerta del Sol. Housing activists plan Spain's first general strike in 15 years on 11 November.

In **Italy**, students in the north are protesting low pay and living costs, while in the south truckers and farmers have blocked motorway exits over fuel prices; the Meloni government sent 500 soldiers to Milan to help keep order. **Belgium, Greece and Albania** have also seen protests of varying size, and Germany has had small protests over education funding.

These movements have no common organisation, but social media carries slogans and tactics across borders. Observers broadly agree that the shared backdrop is a younger generation's disappointment over housing, income and public services.

## 2. Elections: a dense political cycle

- **Serbia (25 October)**: the "Student List", which grew out of the student movement, won the parliamentary election, taking 141 of 250 seats according to Ipsos/CeSID projections. Long-time leader Vučić conceded, and turnout was close to 70%, the highest since 1990. Hungary had already seen a long-serving leader leave office.
- **Spain (29 November)**: Sánchez called a snap election after his housing bill failed in parliament. Most polls suggest the People's Party could form a coalition with the far-right Vox, but the outcome remains uncertain.
- **France (2027 presidential election)**: Marine Le Pen continues to lead the polls, and her "law and order" message is seen as a possible beneficiary of the unrest; still, the first poll after the riots showed little change in voting intentions.
- **Poland**: tension between Tusk's governing coalition and the opposition Law and Justice party (PiS) has sharpened over the suspension of the Constitutional Tribunal's president, with the next parliamentary election about a year away.
- Italy and Greece also face important elections in the period ahead.

## 3. The economy: slowing growth, rising inflation, tight rates

A few recent numbers sketch the eurozone's position:

|---|---|
| Eurozone Q3 GDP (q/q, flash) | +0.4% (expected 0.2%; Q2 +0.6%) |
| Germany / France / Italy / Spain | +0.2% / −0.1% / 0.0% / +0.6% |
| Eurozone September inflation | 3.8% (August 3.2%, a three-year high) |
| ECB deposit rate | 2.50% (held on 29 October; raised 25 bp each in June and September) |
| Eurozone government debt / GDP (end Q2) | 88.8% |
| Eurozone consumer confidence (October) | −17.9 |

Energy is the main driver of inflation. The US–Iran conflict has pushed up oil and gas prices, just as the energy subsidies governments set up for households expire one after another. In its October statement the ECB said the energy shock creates upside risks to inflation and downside risks to growth; so far there are no clear second-round effects in core inflation or wages, but financing conditions have tightened. Borrowing costs in France, Italy, Spain and Greece have risen, squeezing the fiscal room governments have to answer the demands on the street.

## 4. The US midterms: how big a variable?

The US holds its midterm elections on 3 November. Democrats lead the national generic ballot, and most forecasts expect them to win back the House, while the Senate remains close; Trump's average approval rating is about 38%.

For Europe, the impact may be smaller than it looks. Under the earlier EU–US trade deal, most EU goods face a 15% US tariff. Even if Democrats control Congress, the president keeps broad tariff and foreign-policy powers and can veto legislation. EU and NATO officials largely expect US unpredictability not to change fundamentally because of the election. Still, the result will affect US support for Ukraine, sanctions on Russia and the mood of transatlantic trade talks, and it will reach European markets through the dollar and risk appetite.

## 5. What next: possible paths

![The ECB tower on a rainy night](ecb-night.webp)

The following are judgements based on current information, not firm predictions:

1. **Short term (November–December)**: the October flash inflation figure on 4 November is key. If inflation keeps rising, another 25 bp ECB hike at the 17 December meeting becomes more likely, and markets have already partly priced it in. A hike would push southern Europe's financing costs higher still.
2. **Growth**: the eurozone will most likely keep growing slowly. France and Italy risk stagnation or even mild contraction, while Spain and Ireland look relatively strong. Winter energy prices and Germany's low gas storage are the main downside risks.
3. **Politics and budgets**: protests and elections may push governments to spend more on social programmes, but high rates and high debt leave little room. France's public finances draw the most attention, and its bond spread may become a barometer of market sentiment.
4. **The US factor**: if Democrats take Congress, domestic constraints on tariffs may grow, but the US–EU tariff framework is unlikely to change much in the short term; if Republicans keep both chambers, trade policy will be more continuous. Either way, Europe is likely to keep diversifying its defence and trade partners, for example by deepening ties with Canada.

Overall, Europe is in a phase where three pressures overlap: an energy shock, fiscal constraints and an election cycle. Over the next few months, inflation data, the ECB's December decision, Spain's election and the US midterm results will together decide whether this restlessness gradually fades or carries on into France's 2027 presidential election.
